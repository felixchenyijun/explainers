Physical AI, from motion to silicon

Physical AI turns observations into actions in a world that keeps moving: estimate what is happening, predict what might happen, act, and correct—within a deadline.

01 / THE PHYSICAL LOOP

The output changes the next input.

A text model can finish a response. A robot’s action changes its environment, including what it sees next. A small error can become the next input, then a bigger error. Feedback keeps checking the consequences.

“Move to 6 meters” is not a motor command. The machine must estimate position and speed, choose forces, and manage inertia. More force means more acceleration; releasing the force does not instantly stop motion.

A useful architecture separates timescales: a slower task policy proposes motion; a faster controller tracks it. A separate watchdog can reject stale commands. These are design roles; they need not be separate neural networks.

Under the hood: state, force, feedback, and contact equations + implementation intuition

Observation is not state

A camera returns pixels, not velocity, friction, or a trustworthy object pose. Encoders measure joint angles; an IMU measures acceleration and angular rate; tactile sensors reveal local contact. Each has noise, latency, and blind spots. Calibration places them in compatible coordinate frames; timestamps tell you whether the measurements describe the same moment.

st+1 = f(st, at, wt)
ot = h(st, νt)
bt(s) = P(st = s | o≤t, a<t)s: physical state · a: action · o: observation · w: disturbance · ν: sensor noise · b: belief over possible states.

A state estimator combines a prediction with new measurements. A Kalman-style update weights their uncertainty; a recurrent network can encode a useful history in a latent vector. Neither magically observes hidden variables. If an object is occluded, uncertainty should persist or grow. Moving a camera or making gentle contact can gather information: actions can improve the next observation as well as advance the task.

How coordinates become forces

Forward kinematics maps joint angles q to an end-effector pose. Inverse kinematics solves for joint configurations that reach a desired pose. The Jacobian connects small joint motion to tool motion: v = J(q) q̇. Its transpose maps an end-effector wrench into joint torques: τ = J(q)ᵀ F. Singularities, joint limits, collisions, and motor limits constrain what is achievable.

M(q) q̈ + C(q,q̇)q̇ + g(q) = τ + Jc(q)ᵀ λInertia × acceleration + motion-dependent terms + gravity = motor torques + contact forces mapped into joint coordinates. This is a schematic rigid-body equation; friction and actuator dynamics may need extra terms.

That equation is the physical equivalent of a runtime contract: a planner cannot request arbitrary motion. A gripper pushing a cup only controls the cup through contact. Feet cannot pull on the floor. When contact appears or disappears, the effective constraints change. Friction, compliance, slip, and impacts make manipulation much harder than tracing a geometric path. MIT’s dynamics introduction and MuJoCo’s computation model make these constraints concrete.

The exact controller in experiment 01

Our cart has mass m = 1 kg and viscous drag b = 0.35 N·s/m. A smooth, four-second reference moves from 0 to 6 m with zero starting and ending speed and acceleration. Both carts receive the corresponding feedforward force; one also receives a proportional–derivative correction.

Position feedback pulls toward the target. Velocity feedback damps motion. Increasing delay makes the correction refer to the past: the controller can keep pushing after it should brake. This demo uses exact delayed state, no sensor noise, and a fixed controller; it isolates one failure mode rather than modeling a full robot.

What “real time” actually means

Real time means responding before a relevant deadline, not simply running fast on average. As an illustrative architecture, a task policy could update at 5 Hz, a motion policy at 50 Hz, and a joint servo at 1 kHz. These are examples, not universal robot specifications. An action chunk can bridge slow policy updates while the servo continues correcting every millisecond.

For an SWE: the robot resembles a distributed system with timestamped sensor streams, bounded queues, and stale-data failures. But retries do not undo a collision. A deadline miss needs a designed response—such as bounded deceleration or a controlled hold—appropriate to the machine. A language-model instruction is not itself a safety mechanism.

Try futures without living through them.

The useful abstraction is predictive state, not necessarily video. A learned model can roll forward compact latent vectors. It needs to preserve what matters for action: motion, contact, uncertainty, and task consequences. Beautiful generated frames alone do not establish accurate physics.

There are two places to use imagination. Plan online before acting, as in PlaNet; or train an actor on imagined experience, as in DreamerV3. A policy trained with a world model can act without searching futures on every step.

Under the hood: latents, action chunks, training, and sim-to-real from objective to deployment

A compact state for imagination

An encoder turns observations into useful features. A recurrent state integrates history. A learned transition predicts the next latent state given an action; prediction heads estimate observations, reward, or termination. Stochastic latent variables can represent multiple possible futures. A latent is not automatically a calibrated probability distribution or a human-readable physics state.

zt = infer(o≤t, a<t)
zt+1 ∼ pθ(zt+1 | zt, at)
r̂t = rθ(zt, at)A common schematic, not one universal architecture. Reconstruction/prediction losses and latent regularization teach representations; task losses encourage relevant information.

A model that predicts only the common continuation of a video may fail when a robot intervenes. The control test is counterfactual: change the action while holding the starting situation fixed. Does the predicted consequence change correctly? Evaluate multi-step errors, contact events, and decisions under unfamiliar conditions—not just image quality.

A longer planning horizon sees delayed consequences, but costs more compute and compounds prediction error. A longer executed prefix reduces how often you must infer, but leaves you blind to disturbances for longer. These are different knobs. Replanning repairs some errors; it cannot reverse an irreversible collision or make a systematically wrong model trustworthy. MIT’s trajectory optimization notes develop the optimization view.

Our exact toy: a frictionless point cart starts at x = 0 m, v = 1.8 m/s. Model mass is 1 kg. Each plan picks a force in {−2, 0, +2} N for each half of the horizon, giving nine plans. Each segment follows x′ = x + v Δt + ½(u/m)Δt², v′ = v + (u/m)Δt. Only the first 0.2 s is applied to the actual cart.

A real robot does not get that ground-truth future for free: it must compare prediction with later measurements, identify parameters, or learn better dynamics.

How policies learn

Behavior cloning learns actions from demonstrations. A basic objective minimizes negative log likelihood, −log πθ(a | o, instruction). It is efficient when demonstrations cover the task, but the policy’s mistakes can take it into states the demonstrator never visited. Recovery data and on-policy data collection address a different problem from merely collecting more successful trajectories.

Reinforcement learning seeks high expected discounted return, E[Σ γᵗ rₜ]. A critic estimates future return; an actor improves its choices. Reward design, exploration, reset cost, and unsafe trials matter. “Model-free” means the algorithm does not explicitly use an environment dynamics model to plan or train its policy; it can still use huge neural networks and learn from a simulator. Model-based methods use dynamics predictions, trading interaction efficiency against model bias. See the RL algorithm taxonomy.

VLAs connect semantic knowledge to action. A language/vision backbone helps interpret “put the red cup in the drawer,” but robot data grounds those semantics in a particular embodiment and action space. Actions may be joint targets, end-effector deltas, gripper states, or torques. They need consistent units, frames, timing, and limits. RT-2 illustrates action-token learning; VLA architectures are not all identical.

Why generate a chunk of actions?

A policy can output a coordinated sequence, then execute a short prefix before observing again. This amortizes inference and captures temporal structure. A diffusion policy denoises an action sequence conditioned on observations. It is generating actions, not necessarily simulating their physical consequences.

Multimodality matters: going left or right around an obstacle can both work; averaging the two can point straight into it. A generative policy can select a coherent mode. Sampling or denoising iterations consume latency, so faster samplers and distillation can matter at deployment. Diffusion Policy uses action sequences and receding-horizon execution; those facts alone do not imply explicit model-based rollout-and-score planning.

The training loop outside the control loop

demonstrate / explore → collect trajectories → train → evaluate → deploy → collect failures

Training usually runs on larger infrastructure; inference runs wherever the deployed system places its computation. A simulator provides repeatable resets and many parallel trials. A digital twin adds a model of a particular asset or environment; its fidelity still needs validation. Procedural scenes, varied lighting, and dynamics randomization expose policies to more variation.

Sim-to-real is a coverage and modeling problem. Randomizing mass or friction discourages reliance on one exact simulated setting. But a range that omits the real actuator lag will not teach the right response to that lag. Photorealism does not repair incorrect contact dynamics. System identification, calibration, real failure data, and controlled hardware evaluation close parts of the gap. Dynamics randomization research shows a concrete transfer method, not a universal reliability guarantee.

Measure task success together with recovery, unsafe contacts, timing tails, and performance under distribution shift. A higher average success rate can conceal a failure mode that is rare but consequential.

03 / ON-CHIP INFERENCE

A decision is a data-movement problem, too.

On-device inference runs near the sensors and actuators. On-device does not mean every weight stays on silicon. An edge system often keeps large tensors in external DRAM, then streams tiles through on-chip SRAM to a GPU or neural accelerator.

An optimistic lower bound under ideal overlap, not a device benchmark or deadline guarantee. TOPS is held independent of weight storage to isolate compression. Dequantization, operator coverage, scheduling, thermal limits, and contention can add latency.

Quantization saves bytes, with a tradeoff. Fewer bits can reduce weight traffic and enable faster kernels. Accuracy and control behavior must survive the rounding; accumulator precision can stay higher than weight precision.

Local inference avoids a network round trip. That helps latency and resilience, but power, heat, memory, and deadlines still bind. Cloud resources remain useful for training, fleet learning, and tasks that tolerate delay.

The engineering comes together here: better prediction can reduce wasted motion; better feedback limits prediction errors; better scheduling and silicon deliver the correction before it becomes stale.

Under the hood: dot products, rooflines, quantization, and deadlines the arithmetic behind the sliders

What the accelerator actually computes

A dense layer multiplies an activation matrix by a weight matrix: Y = XW. Each output sums products across an inner dimension. Many convolutions and attention operations can use related matrix kernels. A multiply–accumulate (MAC) is often counted as two operations in advertised throughput; compare precision, counting convention, and dense versus sparse assumptions.

SRAM is small and close to the arithmetic; external DRAM holds much more. Tiling organizes work so a loaded operand contributes to many outputs. Register accumulators keep partial sums local. Systolic arrays pass operands between neighboring processing elements; GPU matrix units also exploit tiled reuse. The chip is only busy if data arrives and the workload fits supported kernels. Google’s TPU architecture documentation describes one such design.

Two lower bounds, one critical path

tcompute ≥ operations / peak operations per second
tmemory ≥ bytes transferred / peak bytes per second
tinference ≥ max(tcompute, tmemory)
arithmetic intensity I = operations / bytes
attainable throughput ≤ min(peak compute, bandwidth × I)

This is the roofline intuition: low-intensity work waits for memory; high-intensity work can use more arithmetic. A larger batch often reuses weights across more examples, raising intensity—but waiting to form that batch can be unacceptable for a single robot’s next action.

The calculator assumes all 300M weights are read once per decision, plus 50 MB of other tensor traffic. It uses decimal units. It also assumes 30G operations and ideal overlap of memory transfer with arithmetic. These quantities are independently chosen teaching inputs; they do not describe a named model.

bytes = 300,000,000 × bits / 8 + 50,000,000
8-bit: (300 MB + 50 MB) / 50 GB/s = 7 ms

total ≥ 8 + max(7, 1.5) = 15 msThe synthetic fixed 8 ms represents sensing, preprocessing, control, and output stages. 16-bit → 21 ms; 4-bit → 12 ms with the other inputs unchanged. Real stages need not overlap this well.

At the default, increasing 20 to 40 TOPS halves arithmetic time to 0.75 ms but leaves the seven-millisecond transfer bound untouched. In real deployments, kernels, launch overhead, layout conversions, attention/KV-cache traffic, and shared-memory contention can impose additional limits. Peak TOPS alone cannot tell you end-to-end latency.

What “8-bit” leaves out

q = clip(round(x / scale) + zero_point, qmin, qmax)
x̂ = scale × (q − zero_point)

Quantization maps values onto a finite grid. Scale sets the spacing; bit width, zero point, and scale set the range. Clipping saturates values outside it. Outliers can force a poor compromise. Per-channel scales often preserve more detail. Static activation quantization uses representative calibration data; quantization-aware training simulates quantization during learning so the network can adapt.

Weights, activations, and accumulators can have different types. INT8 products may accumulate into INT32; a packed 4-bit weight may be unpacked or dequantized for a supported kernel. Memory compression does not guarantee an equal arithmetic speedup. TensorRT’s quantization documentation distinguishes formats and execution details.

For physical AI, validate task behavior after conversion, including rare contact and recovery states. A small change in average prediction error may matter disproportionately near a control threshold. Distillation can also train a smaller or faster policy, but it requires behavior-level evaluation.

The runtime is part of the control system

Inference compilation chooses layouts, fuses operators, plans memory, and selects kernels. Unsupported operations can fall back to another processor, introducing transfers and synchronization. CPU scheduling, camera buffers, GPU/NPU contention, and throttling all influence when a command is actually ready.

Measure from sensor capture to actuator command, not just the neural forward pass. Track tail latency and missed deadlines, using the relevant workload and thermal state. An optimistic estimate below 20 ms proves only that a simplified bound fits; it does not prove the deployed system meets the budget.

An edge SoC may combine CPU, GPU, NPU, and image processing blocks. A microcontroller may run a compact model fully from local memory. “On-chip inference” is therefore a placement and dataflow description, not one architecture. Keep the highest-rate loop near the actuator; choose the rest of the placement from actual latency, power, connectivity, and reliability needs.

Check your intuition before you leave five questions, answers inside
  1. Can a good world model act without a policy? It predicts consequences; some decision rule must still choose among them. An optimizer can play that role.
  2. Does a VLA necessarily predict the future? No. It may map observations and instructions directly to actions. Explicit predictive dynamics are a separate property.
  3. Why not execute the whole imagined plan? The actual world diverges. Fresh observations let you repair the next decision; irreversible errors still require prevention.
  4. Why didn’t twice the TOPS cut the default latency? Transfer already took longer than arithmetic. The critical path did not move.
  5. What makes this an engineered system? Compatible representations, timed interfaces, feedback, measured uncertainty, and failure handling connect learning to physical consequences.
