02 / FOLLOW THE ENERGY

Force is carried. Energy is absorbed, stored, and returned.

Your leg is a moving system of muscles, tendons, and tissues. There is no single “shock absorber,” and force does not simply disappear at the knee.

01 / RECEIVE

Spread the landing

Soft tissues deform and muscles control the motion. Some mechanical energy becomes heat; some is briefly stored. The foot and shoe contribute substantially at initial contact. [16]

02 / DISTRIBUTE

Share the contact

Cartilage and menisci help distribute joint load. For the same force, a smaller effective contact area means higher average pressure. An inward shift of load is a distribution issue; it is not proof of failed cushioning. [4]

03 / RETURN

Power the next step

The Achilles tendon stretches and recoils. Muscles also add energy to sustain walking. Bowing does not automatically switch off this mechanism; it may alter how the system carries the load. [17]

Why the inner knee can become an issue

An offset ground force creates an external knee adduction moment. This tends to favor medial loading. Muscles must help balance the moment, and their forces also compress the joint. A larger moment is useful information, but it is not a direct reading of pressure or pain. [5]

04 / WHAT CAN HELP

Build capacity. Adjust load. Treat the cause.

The right option depends on symptoms, strength, joint health, and where the bowing comes from. A comfortable, capable leg may need no treatment for its shape alone.

Strengthening can help without making the legs straight.

Training develops the muscles that control and support movement. It can improve pain and function in knee osteoarthritis. It does not reshape an adult femur or tibia, and bowing alone does not reveal which muscle is weak. [2] [7]

Four muscle groups worth assessing

Mint marks the working area. These are examples to tailor with a physiotherapist—not a prescription to do all four.

01 · HALF SQUAT

Quadriceps + gluteus maximus

Front thigh & buttock

Try: Use a stable support if needed. Lower a little through hips and knees; rise slowly within a comfortable range.

Why: Knee and hip extensors control lowering and help you stand, climb, and accept weight.

Builds movement capacity; it does not guarantee less medial knee load. [10]

02 · SIDE-LYING LEG RAISE

Gluteus medius

Outer hip

Try: Lie on your side, pelvis stacked. Raise the upper leg slightly without rolling backward; lower with control.

Why: Hip abductors help keep the pelvis steady during single-leg support.

Useful when capacity or control is limited. Bowing does not prove weak glutes. [11] [15]

03 · SUPPORTED KNEE CURL

Hamstrings

Back of thigh

Try: Hold a support. Bend one knee to bring the heel behind you, then lower slowly; keep the trunk steady.

Why: Hamstrings flex the knee and contribute to coordinated control with the quadriceps.

The goal is control—not deliberately tensing the thigh all the time while walking. [10] [6]

04 · SUPPORTED HEEL RAISE

Gastrocnemius + soleus

Calf, through the Achilles tendon

Try: Hold a stable support. Raise both heels together, then lower slowly without rolling onto the foot’s outer edge.

Why: The calf–Achilles system contributes to support and push-off; adequate capacity can make these tasks easier.

Not a way to push bowed bones straight or automatically unload the knee. [10] [17]

How to use these examples: Choose the exercise, resistance, range, and progression around an assessment. New sharp pain, swelling, or giving way is a reason to stop and get advice. The diagrams show movement direction, not required depth or a dosing plan. A fixed varus leg should not be forced into an artificial “straight” position.

What about stretching, the “inner quad,” or changing my walking style?

A knee gap does not establish tight adductors, a weak inner quadriceps, or a shortened IT band. Stretching may help a measured restriction, but it cannot straighten mature bones. Gait changes, foot-angle changes, and insoles can alter loads differently between people; choose them after assessment rather than copying a universal correction.

Strengthening is not guaranteed to lower the external knee adduction moment. Better comfort and function can occur without a measurable change in alignment. The muscle explanations above are anatomical rationales—not proof that each exercise prevents arthritis in someone with bow legs. [5] [7]

Other treatments change different parts of the problem.

These are options for selected situations, not a ladder everyone must climb.

CHANGE EXPOSURE

Activity & recovery

Adjust duration, build up gradually, or use cycling and swimming when walking flares symptoms. For people with knee OA and overweight, supported weight management can reduce joint load. Staying active helps retain capacity. [18]

REDISTRIBUTE LOAD

A fitted unloader brace

For selected medial knee OA, a brace applies an opposing moment to shift loading. Fit, comfort, and the actual problem matter. It changes support while worn; it does not permanently reshape bone. [18]

ADD SUPPORT

A walking aid

A properly fitted cane or other aid can make painful walking easier. A cane is generally used opposite the affected leg. A physiotherapist can check height, timing, and balance needs. [19]

MANAGE SYMPTOMS

Pain relief when appropriate

For diagnosed knee OA, a clinician or pharmacist may recommend a topical NSAID. In selected cases, a corticosteroid injection offers short-term relief. These can support activity; neither corrects alignment. Suitability and risks need checking. [7]

CHANGE BONE ALIGNMENT

Osteotomy

A surgeon cuts and repositions bone to move load away from the affected compartment. It can suit selected symptomatic varus knees, often with localized arthritis. It involves bone healing, rehabilitation, and surgical risks; it is not routine for appearance alone. [8]

TREAT ADVANCED JOINT DISEASE

Partial or total replacement

For substantial arthritis with persistent pain and disability despite suitable nonsurgical care, replacement may be considered. It treats damaged joint surfaces and may correct alignment. A bowed shape by itself is not an indication. [18]

The useful assessment question

“Is my problem load tolerance, muscle capacity, instability, damaged joint tissue, or the bone alignment itself—and which finding would change treatment?”

Symptoms, assessment & the bigger picture
PUTTING IT TOGETHER

One alignment. Several possible stories.

Bow legs are a description of shape. They are not, by themselves, a diagnosis of arthritis, a torn meniscus, or a muscle imbalance.

A

Structure

The angle can come from the femur, tibia, or both; joint changes can also contribute. A childhood growth pattern, old injury, or arthritis can have different implications. Rotation is a separate dimension. [1]

B

Capacity

Strength, coordination, recovery, and joint health influence how well you tolerate a given activity. Exercise can improve function even when the underlying adult bone alignment stays the same. [2] [7]

C

Exposure

Symptoms also depend on what you ask the leg to do. Duration, sudden changes in activity, previous injury, and existing osteoarthritis all matter. No slider can turn alignment into your personal risk percentage. [3]

Could this lead to arthritis, meniscus trouble, or pain elsewhere?

Inner-knee osteoarthritis: varus alignment is associated with greater risk of medial-compartment progression in people who already have knee osteoarthritis, and with incident knee osteoarthritis in some cohorts. That is a population association, not a prediction that a particular person will develop it. The underlying study included adults with or at increased risk of osteoarthritis; its risk figures should not be transplanted to a young, symptom-free person. [3]

Meniscus and cartilage: the medial compartment may experience more loading. A meniscus spreads load; when it is damaged, the remaining contact area can become less effective. But a knee gap cannot reveal a tear. Pain, swelling, locking, examination, and sometimes imaging provide the missing information. [4]

Hip, ankle, or outer-knee discomfort: alignment and rotation can alter the demands along the leg. These symptoms have many possible causes, so it is unreliable to blame bow legs automatically. Nor do bow legs guarantee flat feet, back pain, or a specific pattern of tight muscles. [2]

Why “stretch this, strengthen that” is not a diagnosis

A muscle can feel tight because it is short, guarding a painful joint, fatigued, or working hard. These are different problems. A static photograph cannot distinguish them.

Studies in people with knee osteoarthritis describe altered muscle contributions and greater simultaneous activation of opposing muscles in association with varus thrust. They do not establish that every person with bow legs has the same weak or shortened muscles. [5] [6]

A useful examination checks knee and hip motion, strength, joint stability, foot and ankle motion, and how the whole limb behaves under load. A targeted plan follows those findings.

FROM THE MODEL TO YOUR BODY

Find out what is true for you.

A physiotherapist, sports-medicine clinician, or orthopaedic specialist can connect alignment to your actual symptoms and goals.

Bring a useful description

Where does it hurt? During which activity? Is there swelling, locking, or giving way? Is one side different? Has the shape changed? Mention injuries and what you want to keep doing.

“Is my alignment mainly bony, rotational, or dynamic? Which structures explain my symptoms? What would change the treatment plan?”

Understand the assessment

When indicated, a standing full-length hip-to-ankle radiograph measures the mechanical axis and helps locate the deformity. A knee-only image or phone photo cannot supply the same information. MRI answers different questions about soft tissues. [1] [4]

Persistent pain, swelling, reduced function, or increasing/asymmetric bowing deserve assessment. If you are still growing, an adult guide is not the right basis for care. [1]

What can improve—and what changes the bone alignment?
Strength and activity

Individualized strengthening and aerobic activity can improve pain and function in knee osteoarthritis. A clinician can adapt exercise to your symptoms and capacity. Bow legs alone are not a reason to assume all running or squatting is forbidden. [7]

Supports

Selected braces or other aids may help some people with abnormal loading or instability; they are not an automatic prescription for every bowed leg. They do not permanently reshape mature bones. [7]

Bone realignment

An osteotomy changes alignment by cutting and repositioning bone. It is an option for selected patients after assessment of symptoms, joint condition, and deformity—not an inevitable next step. It involves recovery and surgical risks; the illustration is not a surgical plan. [8]

When to get prompt help

A hot, red knee with fever; sudden major swelling or deformity; inability to bear weight; or a knee that is locked or repeatedly gives way needs prompt medical assessment. NHS guidance ↗

Research checked September 29, 2026. Educational synthesis, not a clinical assessment. The source studies and patient guides have different scopes.

  1. HSS · Bowlegs: symptoms, causes, diagnosis and treatment ↗Clinical overview; causes and standing alignment imaging.
  2. HSS · Bowlegs in adults ↗Structural alignment and rotation. A surgical-service perspective, not proof that everyone needs surgery.
  3. Sharma et al., 2010 · Alignment and incident/progressive knee osteoarthritis ↗Longitudinal observational cohort. Associations do not determine an individual outcome.
  4. AAOS · Meniscus tears ↗Meniscal anatomy, symptoms, examination, and imaging.
  5. Sritharan et al., 2018 · Lower-limb muscle function during gait in varus mal-aligned OA ↗Musculoskeletal modeling in 39 patients and 15 controls; not a strain map of all bow-legged people.
  6. Dixon et al., 2018 · Muscle co-contraction and varus thrust ↗Association in people with knee osteoarthritis; does not establish causation.
  7. NICE NG226 · Osteoarthritis management ↗Tailored therapeutic exercise and selective use of supports. Applies to osteoarthritis, not a diagnosis of it.
  8. AAOS · Osteotomy of the knee ↗Realignment surgery, patient selection, benefits, and limitations.
  9. NHS · Knee pain ↗When symptoms need urgent attention.
  10. AAOS · Knee conditioning program ↗Muscle groups used in knee rehabilitation; an exercise handout does not replace an assessment.
  11. NCBI Bookshelf · Gluteus medius anatomy ↗Anatomical function and pelvic stabilization.
  12. NCBI Bookshelf · Femoral muscles ↗Thigh muscle compartments and the iliotibial tract.
  13. AAOS · Collateral ligaments ↗Side-to-side restraint and ligament injury assessment.
  14. NCBI Bookshelf · Quadriceps anatomy ↗Four muscle heads, knee extension, and the extensor mechanism.
  15. AAOS · Hip conditioning program ↗Hip-abductor exercise examples; select exercises with a clinician.
  16. Honert & Zelik, 2019 · Foot and shoe contributions to energy absorption ↗Biomechanics experiment; helps explain soft-tissue work after foot contact.
  17. Zelik et al., 2014 · Ankle elasticity in walking ↗How elastic energy storage and return influence walking energetics.
  18. AAOS · Arthritis of the knee ↗Activity, supports, and joint replacement for arthritis; not a diagnosis based on bowing.
  19. South Tees NHS · Using a walking stick ↗Opposite-hand use and coordination with the affected leg.
  20. Kutzner et al., 2010 · Knee loading during daily activities ↗Direct measurements in five people with instrumented knee implants. Useful task comparison; not normal native-knee values or a risk threshold.
  21. Heller et al., 2003 · Alignment and dynamic knee loading ↗A model varying alignment in four individuals during walking and stair climbing. Highly individual modeled responses.
  22. Verlaan et al., 2019 · Adduction moments during stairs ↗People with and without knee OA at self-selected speed. No significant group differences in normalized moment peaks or impulse.
  23. Kutzner et al., 2017 · Load sharing across activities ↗Nine instrumented knee implants: alignment correlated with medial force share in single-leg support; two-leg tasks with more flexion shifted force laterally. Force share is not total medial force or native-knee injury risk.
  24. Hakukawa et al., 2022 · Knee varus and three-dimensional ankle alignment ↗Standing upright-CT study: 45 limbs in 25 people with knee OA. Associations vary by OA grade; not proof of ankle pain in every bowed leg.
  25. Wallace et al., 2002 · Kneecap loading during squats ↗Biomechanical estimates in 15 healthy adults, with and without resistance. Flexion and load changed demand; no bow-leg injury threshold was tested.
  26. Lorenzetti et al., 2018 · Stance, foot angle and squat mechanics ↗42 novice and experienced squatters. Technique changes joint moments; does not prescribe one ideal stance for bow legs.
  27. Hunt et al., 2008 · Trunk lean and dynamic knee load ↗Cross-sectional gait study in 120 people with varus knee OA. Does not show that bow legs cause hip or back injury.
  28. Lun et al., 2004 · Static alignment and running injury ↗Prospective study of 87 recreational runners. Overall static alignment did not distinguish injured from uninjured runners; injury-specific subgroup findings were limited.
  29. Kutzner et al., 2012 · Knee loading during cycling ↗Nine instrumented knee implants. Tested cycling forces were lower than walking and changed with power, cadence and seat height.
  30. Kutzner et al., 2017 · Joint loading in aquatic exercise ↗Instrumented hip and knee implants in 12 people. Water buoyancy reduces support demand; faster movement and drag still add muscle-generated loading.
How to read this model · assumptions and technical credits

The neutral reference places each hip, knee, and ankle center on a line in the frontal plane. The varus control shifts each knee outward while holding the hip and ankle centers fixed. It illustrates a hip–knee–ankle deviation from neutral, not a measured femoral or tibial deformity. A real deformity can occur at different levels, include rotation, and differ between legs.

The hip-to-ankle line is a geometric mechanical axis. It is not the instantaneous ground-reaction-force vector. The load lesson separately illustrates how a force acting at an offset creates a moment. No force percentages, contact pressures, muscle activation values, or clinical risk estimates are computed.

The joint view uses an artificial separation to reveal cartilage and menisci. Its surfaces remain intact at every angle; moving the slider does not imply tissue damage. Muscle paths and attachment areas are schematic. The original atlas motion example isolates outward knee excursion. The walking lab adds procedural, schematic whole-body gait with an approximately 60% stance phase. Its force curve and red intensity are illustrative; they are not measured muscle activation, joint contact, tissue strain, or pain. The walking slider changes a lateral knee offset, not a calibrated clinical HKA angle. The movement library uses original procedural keyframes to compare the same task at two schematic alignments; its 18 families are not a complete catalogue of human motion. Red shows potential medial load bias during support. Amber identifies shared demand or possible compensation. Neither is a calculated strain, contact force, injury probability, or pain map. Activity-specific measurements, studies in knee OA, studies of implanted knees, and mechanistic inferences are explicitly distinguished. The library does not model how an individual would adapt their technique. The force diagram enlarges offsets for clarity; a neutral alignment can still have an adduction moment during real walking.
